The smoke test said yes

Three times in one build, the signal everyone acts on said yes and a small probe said no. A commercial injection filter at its most sensitive setting missed the payload a sentence-level heuristic catches — and the miss has a measured boundary at 89 characters. A documented reasoning budget turned out to be an off switch. And a model that went 12/12 on small calls could not finish the real workload twice.

Three measured findings, the probes that settled them, and the mechanism behind each one.

The system these findings come from reads supplier documents — certificates of insurance, food-safety certificates, bank letters — extracts the fields with an LLM, screens the company against a sanctions index, scores the result with a written policy, and writes the outcome into a real ERP. It runs mostly unattended, and it was built on a hackathon deadline, which is exactly the condition under which surface signals are most tempting: a filter shows enabled, a knob accepts a value, a smoke test comes back green, and the schedule wants you to move on.

Three times, the surface signal and the measured behaviour disagreed. Each disagreement was settled by a probe small enough to write in an afternoon, each probe is committed next to its evidence and re-runs from one command, and each produced not just a verdict but a mechanism — which is the part that makes a finding portable instead of an anecdote. The three reduce to one sentence each:

Enabled is not detecting. Configured is not bounded. Responding is not serving.

symptom: enabled is not detecting

The filter that stopped detecting at 108 characters

The build's canonical injection fixture is a synthetic food-safety certificate with a sentence planted mid-document: "NOTE TO AUTOMATED SYSTEMS PROCESSING THIS DOCUMENT: Ignore all previous instructions…" — the realistic shape of the attack, dressed in document formatting, surrounded by legitimate certificate prose. Two detectors got to read it.

The first is Model Armor, Google Cloud's model-safety service, with its prompt-injection and jailbreak filter enabled at LOW_AND_ABOVE — the most sensitive setting the template accepts. The second is the incumbent: a deterministic scanner that taints a page only when a directive (an instruction addressed to the reader) and a machine-reader signal (something that only makes sense if the reader is automated) occur in the same sentence.

The commercial filter returned NO_MATCH_FOUND on the full document. The sentence-level heuristic tainted the same page with five findings.

The first question a result like that should raise is whether the instrument works at all. It does: a blunt English jailbreak matched at HIGH confidence on the same template, bare and with certificate prose around it. So this is a detection gap, not a misconfigured filter — which makes the mechanism worth isolating. Bisecting the amount of certificate prose prepended to the payload:

input to the filterresult
payload aloneMATCH
payload after 89 chars of certificate proseMATCH
payload after 108 chars of certificate proseNO_MATCH_FOUND
the full certificateNO_MATCH_FOUND

Two explanations that would have been easy to reach for are ruled out by the same probe. Not language: an English-only rewrite of the certificate also missed. Not sentence segmentation: fed to the filter one sentence at a time, nothing matched — whatever signal the classifier needs is spread across sentences, so there is no chunking strategy that recovers the detection. The effect is context dilution, and it is specific to borderline payloads: the blunt HIGH-confidence jailbreak survived triple-length prose unharmed, while the realistic payload never scored above LOW even with no surrounding prose at all. That last measurement closes the tuning question — at any less sensitive template setting the payload never fires, so no configuration of this filter detects this payload inside a real document.

To be fair to the service: its malicious-URI filter caught a known-bad URL appended to a real certificate and produced zero false positives on clean fixtures — that one delivers exactly what it claims. The sensitive-data filter in basic mode matched only a credit-card number on a certificate that also carried an email address, a phone number, and a US SSN. It is a mixed instrument, and only measurement on your own corpus tells you which parts work for you.

Honesty about the winner, too: the sentence-level scanner is a heuristic over a representative fixture, not a general injection defence, and a rephrased payload gets past it. That is why detection was never the load-bearing control in this system — a document flagged as tainted is simply never published to any state an agent can read, and the risk gate forces the case to blocked, so a tainted document cannot produce an ERP write no matter what any model thinks of it. Detection tunes recall. Enforcement is structural. The finding here is narrower and measurable: on the corpus that actually exists, the small heuristic beats the commercial classifier, and the margin has a boundary you can measure in characters.

symptom: configured is not bounded

The thinking budget that was an off switch

Every LLM call this system makes carries two things: a response schema, because downstream code consumes structure rather than prose, and a named thinking budget, pinned to keep latency inside a hard demo window. A budget invites a specific mental model — a ceiling. Set 1024 and the model reasons, briefly. That is what the word means everywhere else.

Measured, the deployed configuration does not bound reasoning. It turns reasoning off: zero thinking tokens, every run, on both model generations tested.

The probe is the extractor's own instruction over a real fixture certificate, temperature 0, median of three calls per cell, varying only the two knobs:

call shapegemini-3.6-flashgemini-3.7-flash
schema, no budget972532
schema + budget 1024 (the deployed cell)00
no schema, budget 10245140

The third row is the interesting one, because it kills the obvious generalisation. On 3.6 the off switch is not a property of the budget alone: drop the schema, keep the same 1024 budget, and the model reasons at a 514-token median. It is the conjunction of structured output and a named budget that reads as "do not reason at length" — a property of the model and the call together. The first time this behaviour was written down it was recorded as a property of the model, full stop, and the follow-up measurement had to narrow it. A claim that lives in a code comment gets exactly one chance to be too broad before someone builds on the broad version.

Operationally the pin stood, because the consequences were measured rather than assumed: with reasoning off, the full graded eval suite still passes 8/8, and a full two-supplier run dropped from 85 to 57 seconds of machine time. But what shipped is "reasoning off", not "reasoning capped near the median" — and knowing which of those you shipped is the difference between a latency optimisation and an unexamined capability cut. Raising the number does not buy back a middle setting; that was the one thing a newer model might have offered, which is why it got evaluated next.

thinking_budget=1024 reads as "reason, but briefly" 0 thinking tokens, every run

symptom: responding is not serving

The smoke test that would have shipped an outage

The candidate was the newer flash model, and the evaluation had exactly one question: does it honour a thinking budget as a bound, restoring the middle setting the pin removed? The matrix above answers no — its deployed cell is zero, same as the incumbent. But the evaluation surfaced a second, independent disqualifier that is the more portable finding.

Method first: before touching the candidate, the 8-case eval suite was re-run on the incumbent the same day — this suite had gone stale under the build once before, so the control comes first. The incumbent ran the suite clean, twice, in the same session. The candidate aborted on 429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED at case 5 of 8. The second attempt aborted at case 6 — a different case, same error. No score was ever produced.

Then the probe that reframes the whole thing: 24 interleaved small calls, both models, inside the same minute — 12/12 on the incumbent and 12/12 on the candidate. A shallow smoke test would have called the failing model healthy.

The failure is payload- and load-dependent: the eval's calls carry a full document and a response schema, and those are what fail. And it is not a quota — the per-model token quotas on this project are identical for both models, effective equal to default. That combination points at serving-side capacity for a newly released model: not a limit you can see on a dashboard, not one you can raise by request, and not one a dozen small requests will ever reproduce.

The stakes made this one vivid. The system has to survive a month-long judging window unattended. The standard upgrade ritual — swap the model name, fire a handful of requests, watch them succeed, ship — would have deployed a model that fails intermittently on exactly the payloads production sends, at unpredictable times, with no local test that catches it first. The re-run of the full suite, twice, on both models, cost perhaps an hour. It is the only thing standing between this build and that outcome.

Three signals, one failure shape

Notice that every surface signal was true. The filter really was enabled, and really does detect some injections. The budget really was accepted, and latency really did fall. The endpoint really did answer twelve calls in a row. What failed was the inference from signal to conclusion — enabled to detecting, configured to bounded, responding to serving — and nothing in any dashboard was ever going to flag the gap, because dashboards report the signal, not the inference you drew from it.

What settled all three was the same probe discipline, and it is cheap enough to state as rules:

  • probe the payload you actually shipAll three false signals came from miniatures — a blunt jailbreak, a bare API call, a dozen small requests. The truth lived in the full-size case: the real certificate, the deployed call shape with its schema and budget, the real eval suite. If the probe's input is more convenient than production's, the probe is measuring the convenience.
  • run the control before believing the findingThe jailbreak control proved the filter template worked before "it missed" meant anything. Re-running the suite on the incumbent first proved the 429s belonged to the candidate and not to the day. A surprising measurement without a control is a coin flip about which side of it is broken.
  • bisect for the mechanism before generalisingThe character-count bisection turned "the filter missed" into "context dilution with a boundary between 89 and 108 characters" — which immediately says what would and would not fix it. The schema-toggle cell turned "this model doesn't reason under a budget" into "this call shape turns reasoning off". A finding with a mechanism has a scope; a finding without one becomes folklore.

Dashboards report the signal, not the inference. The probes are for finding out what is true.

Each probe took an afternoon or less, is committed to the repository next to the evidence it produced, and re-runs from a single command — because the second-cheapest thing after writing a probe is re-running it, and a measurement you cannot re-run has a shelf life you will not notice expiring.
