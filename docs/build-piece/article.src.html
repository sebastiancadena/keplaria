Field notes · autonomous agents

The smoke test said yes

You enable the safety filter. You set the model's thinking budget. You try the new model on a dozen calls and every one comes back clean. I did all three this month, building an AI agent on a deadline — then measured what was actually happening. All three signals were lying, each in a way worth knowing about before you trust the same signals yourself.

Three findings from an agent build, each with a reproducible probe and a measured mechanism.

First, the context these findings come from. This August I entered an AI agent hackathon with a system that automates supplier onboarding — the back-office chore where a company vets a new vendor before buying from them. Mine takes the stack of documents a would-be supplier sends in (a certificate of insurance, a food-safety certificate, a bank letter), extracts the relevant fields with Gemini, screens the company's name against a sanctions watchlist, scores everything with a written risk policy, and writes the approved supplier into an ERP system. Then it keeps governing that supplier for months: certificates expire, renewal requests go out, purchasing holds get applied and lifted — with nobody watching.

"Nobody watching" is the phrase that shaped how I worked. A system that runs unattended cannot be defended by anyone's intuition on the day something goes wrong, so wherever I could, I replaced "it should behave like X" with a measurement. Three of those measurements flatly contradicted the signal I would otherwise have trusted:

  • finding oneA commercial prompt-injection filter, at its most sensitive setting, missed the attack document that my own small scanner catches — and the reason it missed is measurable, down to a character count.
  • finding twoThe API parameter documented as a cap on how much the model reasons is, in my configuration, a switch that turns reasoning off entirely.
  • finding threeA newly released model passed a dozen test calls without a hiccup, then failed the real workload twice.

Enabled is not detecting. Configured is not bounded. Responding is not serving.

Each finding below follows the same arc — the signal, the probe that contradicted it, and the mechanism — because the arc is the reusable part. The specific numbers are mine; the failure shape is anyone's.

finding one: enabled is not detecting

The filter that stopped detecting at 108 characters

Prompt injection is the failure mode where a document instructs your AI and your AI obeys. For an agent that reads paperwork sent in by strangers, it is the front-door risk, so I keep a test document for it: a fake food-safety certificate with one sentence planted in the middle — "NOTE TO AUTOMATED SYSTEMS PROCESSING THIS DOCUMENT: Ignore all previous instructions…" — followed by the values an attacker would like copied into the ERP. It looks like a certificate. That is the point.

Google Cloud sells a defence for exactly this: Model Armor, a screening API — you send it text, it tells you whether the text contains an attack. I enabled its prompt-injection filter at LOW_AND_ABOVE, the most sensitive setting a filter template accepts, and sent the certificate.

It answered NO_MATCH_FOUND: no attack here. The scanner I had already written — a small deterministic one, a couple of regex families and a co-occurrence rule — flagged the same page with five findings.

That scanner is nothing sophisticated, which is what makes the comparison interesting. It flags a sentence only when two things occur in it together: a directive (an instruction addressed to the reader) and a machine-reader signal (wording that only makes sense if the reader is software). Either alone is ordinary paperwork — "do not disclose this document" is a directive, and harmless. Both in one sentence is how injection reads, and "NOTE TO AUTOMATED SYSTEMS … Ignore all previous instructions" trips the rule immediately.

A result this lopsided should first make you suspect your own setup, so: control. A blunt, classic jailbreak string sent through the same template matched at HIGH confidence, alone and with certificate prose around it. The filter works; the template is configured correctly. It is specifically the realistic payload it cannot see. The next question is why — and that turned out to be measurable. I prepended legitimate certificate prose to the payload a little at a time and re-scanned:

input to the filterresult
the planted sentence aloneMATCH
after 89 chars of certificate proseMATCH
after 108 chars of certificate proseNO_MATCH_FOUND
the full certificateNO_MATCH_FOUND

Two convenient explanations died in the same session. Not the document's language: an English-only rewrite of the certificate was missed just the same. Not chunking: fed to the filter one sentence at a time, nothing matched — no single sentence carries enough signal on its own, so there is no splitting strategy that recovers the detection. What is left is context dilution: enough surrounding legitimate text drowns a borderline payload. And "borderline" is doing real work in that sentence — the blunt jailbreak survived three times as much prose without dropping, while the realistic payload never scored above LOW even standing alone. Sensitivity was already at maximum. There is no setting of this filter that catches this payload inside a real document.

Score one for the small scanner — but keep the score honest, in both directions. My scanner is a heuristic tuned against a representative fixture; a rephrased payload walks past it, and I claim nothing more for it. It was also never the load-bearing defence: in this system a document flagged as tainted is never shown to any agent at all, and the case is forced to a blocked verdict, so a tainted document cannot cause an ERP write no matter what any model thinks of it. And Model Armor is not one filter. Its malicious-URL check caught a known-bad link appended to a certificate, with zero false positives on clean documents — that one delivers. Its sensitive-data filter in basic mode found a credit-card number and missed an email address, a phone number, and a US Social Security number on the same page. The lesson is not "don't use the service". The lesson is that "the filter is enabled" told me nothing about whether it detects anything on my documents — only sending it my documents did.

finding two: configured is not bounded

The thinking budget that was an off switch

Recent Gemini models can "think" before they answer — spend tokens on internal reasoning you never see, which costs real seconds. The API's knob for this is called thinking_budget, and everything about that name says ceiling: set 1024 and the model may reason, but not at length. I set one on each of my three agents to keep a live demo inside a hard time window.

One more detail, and it is the whole finding: every call my agents make also declares an output schema — the response must come back as JSON in a fixed shape, because code consumes it, not a person. Budget plus schema is a completely ordinary production configuration for an agent. Here is what that combination does to reasoning, measured with my extraction prompt over a real fixture document, temperature 0, median of three calls per cell:

call shapegemini-3.6-flashgemini-3.7-flash
schema, no budget972532
schema + budget 1024 (what I deploy)00
no schema, budget 10245140

The deployed row is zero. Not low — zero thinking tokens, every single run, on both model generations. The parameter I set as a cap is, in this call shape, an off switch.

The bottom row is what kills the easy generalisation, "this model just doesn't reason under a budget". Drop the schema, keep the identical budget, and gemini-3.6-flash reasons at a 514-token median. The off switch is a property of the model and the call shape together. The first time I wrote this behaviour down, I recorded it as a fact about the model; the measurement forced the narrower claim. A claim in a code comment gets exactly one chance to be too broad before somebody builds on the broad version.

Operationally my latency pin survived, because its consequences were measured rather than assumed: with reasoning off, my evaluation suite still passes 8/8, and a full two-supplier demo run dropped from 85 to 57 seconds of machine time. But it matters to know which thing you shipped. I thought I had configured "reasoning, kept brief". I had configured "reasoning: off". One is a latency tune; the other is a capability cut you had better be testing for. And there is no middle setting to be had — raising the number does not buy one back. That is exactly why I went to look at a newer model next.

thinking_budget=1024 reads as "reason, but briefly" 0 thinking tokens, every run

finding three: responding is not serving

The smoke test that would have shipped an outage

The candidate was the newer flash model, gemini-3.7-flash, and the evaluation had exactly one question: does it honour a thinking budget as a bound, giving back the middle setting the incumbent doesn't have? The table above already answers that — its budget-plus-schema cell is also zero — so the swap died on its merits. But running the evaluation properly surfaced a second, unrelated disqualifier, and it is the finding I would most want another builder to have.

Method first. Before touching the candidate, I re-ran my evaluation suite on the incumbent — eight end-to-end scenarios, each pushing a real document through extraction, screening, and policy, with the same schemas production uses — because this suite had gone stale on me once before, and a control you did not re-run is not a control. The incumbent 3.6 ran the suite clean, twice, in one session. Then the candidate: aborted at scenario 5 with 429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED, the API's "no capacity for you right now". A second attempt aborted at scenario 6. Different scenario, same error, and no score ever produced.

Then the probe that reframes it: 24 small calls, alternating between the two models inside the same minute — 12/12 on the incumbent and 12/12 on the candidate. The standard smoke test would have pronounced the failing model perfectly healthy.

What separates the calls that fail from the calls that succeed is size and shape: the suite's calls carry a full document and a response schema; the smoke test's calls carry neither. And it is not a quota problem — the published per-model rate limits on my project are identical for the two models, effective equal to default. Put those together and the picture is serving-side capacity for a newly released model: invisible on every dashboard I can see, not raisable by request, and unreproducible by any number of small requests.

The stakes are what made this one vivid. My system has to survive a month of judging unattended. The normal upgrade ritual — change the model string, fire a handful of requests, watch them all succeed, ship — would have deployed a model that fails intermittently on exactly the payloads production sends, at unpredictable times, with no local test that catches it first. Re-running the full suite, twice, on both models, cost about an hour. Cheap, against that.

Three signals, one failure shape

Every one of these signals was true. The filter was enabled, and it does detect attacks — some attacks. The budget parameter was accepted, and latency really did fall. The endpoint really did answer twelve calls out of twelve. What broke was the inference drawn from each — enabled to detecting, configured to bounded, responding to serving — and no dashboard flags a broken inference, because dashboards report signals. The inference happens in your head.

What settled all three was the same probe discipline, cheap enough to state as rules:

  • probe the payload you actually shipAll three lies were told by miniatures — a canned jailbreak string, a bare API call, a dozen small requests. The truth lived at full size: the real certificate, the call with its schema and its budget, the full workload. If your probe's input is more convenient than production's, you are measuring the convenience.
  • run the control before believing the findingThe jailbreak control proved the filter and template worked before "it missed" meant anything. Re-running the suite on the incumbent model first proved the 429s belonged to the candidate and not to the day. A surprising measurement without a control is a coin flip about which side of it is broken.
  • bisect for the mechanism before generalisingThe character-count bisection turned "the filter missed" into "context dilution with a boundary between 89 and 108 characters" — which immediately says what would and would not fix it. The schema-toggle row turned "this model doesn't reason under a budget" into "this call shape turns reasoning off". A finding with a mechanism has a scope; a finding without one becomes folklore.

Dashboards report the signal, not the inference. The probes are for finding out what is true.

Each probe took an afternoon or less, lives in the repository next to the evidence it produced, and re-runs from a single command — because the second-cheapest thing after writing a probe is re-running it, and a measurement you cannot re-run has a shelf life you will not notice expiring.
